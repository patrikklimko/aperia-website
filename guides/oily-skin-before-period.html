Oily skin before your period: why your face gets greasy (and what to log)

Oily skin before your period is one of the most common cycle changes people notice. Your T-zone shines by lunchtime, makeup slides, and your hair looks greasy a day early. Here's what your cycle is doing, why the shift can feel sudden, and how to track your own oil pattern.

Why skin gets oily before your period

Your skin makes oil (sebum) in small glands attached to your hair follicles. How much they make isn't fixed. It shifts with your hormones, and your hormones shift across your cycle.

A typical cycle runs about 28 days, though anywhere from 21 to 35 days can be normal [1]. Estrogen rises in the first half. Around the middle, a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) releases an egg. After that, in the luteal phase, progesterone rises. Late in the cycle, estrogen and progesterone both drop and your period starts [1].

Androgens turn up the oil

Everyone has androgens, such as testosterone. Your oil glands respond to them, and androgens push the glands to grow and make more sebum [6]. In a study that measured hormones at three points in the cycle, testosterone and free testosterone peaked at mid-cycle and stayed higher in the middle of the luteal phase than in the early follicular phase [2]. The change is modest. Your oil glands may still notice it.

What the skin measurements show

In a small study, researchers measured women's skin every week for two to three months. Skin surface oil appeared highest on days 16 to 20 of the cycle. The skin barrier looked weakest between days 22 and 26, and skin was driest in the first days of the cycle [3].

That pattern fits what many people describe: oil building after ovulation, then skin feeling shinier and more reactive in the run-up to a period. It's a small study of one group of women, so think of it as a rough map, not a rule. Your own timing may be a few days off in either direction.

Pre-period shine is often a timing story. Oil builds after ovulation, and you notice it most in the days before you bleed.

When the oil usually shows up

Most people don't track oil day by day, so they notice it once it's obvious. For many, that's the week before their period. On a typical 28-day cycle, that's roughly days 22 to 28, which the cycle skin calculator calls your "watch week".

But the climb often starts earlier. If the study above holds for you, oil may already be rising in the early luteal phase, just after ovulation [3]. Some people first notice it around ovulation itself.

Oil also matters for breakouts. More sebum plus built-up skin cells is the classic setup for clogged pores [6]. That's one reason oily days and pre-period breakouts often arrive together. In one survey of adult women seeing a dermatologist, 65% said their breakouts got worse around their period. Of those, 56% said the week before was the worst [4].

Why it can feel so sudden

Lots of people search "why is my skin so oily all of a sudden". When it happens before a period, there are a few reasons it can feel abrupt:

  • You notice the peak, not the climb. A slow rise over a week or more is easy to miss until your forehead shines by noon.
  • Several changes stack up. More oil, a skin barrier that may be weaker [3] and the first congested pores can all land in the same few days.
  • Your routine fights back. When skin feels greasy, it's tempting to scrub or switch to a stronger cleanser. A harsh face wash can irritate skin and trigger more oil [5], which can make the next day feel worse.

If the oil isn't tied to your cycle, look at what else changed. Hot or humid weather, a new moisturizer, sunscreen or makeup, a stressful stretch or a run of short nights can all play a part. In one small study of students, breakouts got worse during exam stress [7]. None of these follow your cycle, so a few weeks of notes help separate them.

Hormonal birth control can change the picture too. Starting, stopping or switching methods may change how your skin behaves. If that happened recently, note the date.

Oily skin and hair before your period

Many people notice a greasy face and greasy hair on the same days. That makes sense, as your scalp has plenty of oil glands too. If you usually go two or three days between washes, you might find your hair needs washing a day sooner in the week before your period.

The research on cycle changes in scalp oil is thin, so we can't give you a number. It's still worth logging. "Washed hair a day early" is an easy, honest signal, and it's often easier to spot than subtle changes on your face.

What about oily skin after your period?

For many people, oil settles once their period starts. In the same weekly study, skin was driest between days 1 and 6 [3]. That's one reason some people feel their skin looks better on their period.

That isn't everyone's pattern, though. If your skin stays oily after your period:

  • you may simply have oilier skin all month, with smaller ups and downs
  • spots that started forming before your period can still surface afterwards (see breaking out after your period)
  • non-cycle factors like weather, products, stress or sleep may be doing more than your hormones

Only your own notes can tell you which applies.

What to log to see your oil pattern

Oiliness is surprisingly hard to judge from memory. A quick daily log over two or three cycles turns "I think I get oily before my period" into something you can actually see. Keep it simple:

  1. Blotting paper test. At the same time each day, say mid-afternoon, press a blotting sheet gently on your forehead, nose and chin for a few seconds. Don't rub. Note whether it comes away clean, a little see-through or soaked. The American Academy of Dermatology suggests the same press-don't-rub technique for blotting [5].
  2. T-zone shine, 1–5. Before you wash or add makeup, give your forehead, nose and chin a quick score from 1 (matte) to 5 (very shiny).
  3. A daily scan or photo. Same time, same light, bare face. In Aperia, your daily face scan gives an oil score by zone, so you can see shine change across the month instead of guessing.
  4. Your cycle day. Day 1 is the first day of full bleeding. When your next period starts, look back at which days were oiliest.
  5. Sleep. Roughly how long you slept, and whether it was broken.
  6. Stress. A simple low, medium or high is enough.
  7. Hair. Whether your hair needed washing sooner than usual.
  8. Anything new. Products, weather, travel, or a change in birth control.

After a couple of cycles, look for a pattern. Does shine climb from a certain cycle day? Is it worse after short nights? Does it only happen in summer? That's the information that helps you plan ahead, and it's useful to bring to a dermatologist.

A gentle routine for oily days

The goal isn't to strip every bit of oil. Oil helps protect your skin, and the aim is balance [5]. A few simple habits from dermatologists suit pre-period days well [5]:

  • Cleanse gently, up to twice a day and after sweating. A mild, foaming cleanser is enough. Skip scrubbing, even when your skin feels greasy.
  • Don't go harsher. Strong or alcohol-based cleansers can irritate skin, and irritation can mean more oil.
  • Keep moisturizing. Oily skin still needs moisture. A light, oil-free moisturizer works for many people.
  • Look for "oil-free" and "non-comedogenic" on moisturizers, sunscreen and makeup. Labels like these mean a product is less likely to clog pores.
  • Blot, don't powder over and over. Press a blotting sheet on shiny areas through the day.
  • Hands off. Touching your face spreads oil and dirt around.
  • Take makeup off before bed, every night.

Since the oilier days often repeat, plan for them. Keep your routine steady all month rather than overhauling it the moment shine appears. For a phase-by-phase approach, see a skincare rhythm for every phase of your cycle.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

A monthly rhythm of oilier and less oily days is common and usually nothing to worry about. Talk to a doctor or dermatologist if:

  • your skin became much oilier suddenly and stayed that way
  • oily skin comes with deep, painful or scarring breakouts
  • your cycles are very irregular, or you often go months without a period
  • you notice other changes too, like new hair growth on your face or body
  • your skin is affecting how you feel day to day

A few months of notes on oil, cycle day, sleep and stress can make that appointment more useful.

Questions

Why is my skin so oily the week before my period?

After ovulation, your body shifts into the luteal phase. Testosterone tends to stay a little higher than early in the cycle, and androgens stimulate your oil glands. In one small study, skin surface oil was highest around days 16 to 20 of the cycle. Many people notice that extra shine carrying into the days before their period.

Why is my skin so oily all of a sudden?

If it happens around the same point every month, your cycle is a likely part of the story. Other common reasons include hot or humid weather, a harsh cleanser that leaves skin irritated, a new product, stress or short sleep. If the change is sudden and lasting, or comes with other changes like irregular periods or new hair growth, see a doctor or dermatologist.

Is oily skin and hair before my period normal?

Many people notice both at once. Your scalp has oil glands too, so a shinier face and hair that looks greasier sooner can show up on the same days. It's a common pattern. Logging both for a couple of cycles shows whether it lines up with your cycle for you.

Why is my skin oily after my period?

For many people, oil is lower in the first days after a period, but not for everyone. Some people simply have oilier skin all month, and spots that started forming before your period can still surface afterwards. Weather, products and stress don't follow your cycle either, so log them alongside your skin.

Can oily skin before my period tell me if I'm pregnant?

No. Skin changes can't tell you whether you're pregnant; a test can.
