Same pimple, same spot, every period: what's going on

Getting a pimple in the same spot every period can feel oddly personal, like your skin has a favourite place to break out. There are usually ordinary reasons behind it. Here's what may be going on, what face maps get wrong, and how to track your own pattern.

Why the same spot keeps coming back

If you get a pimple in the same spot every period, you've already spotted a useful pattern. Most of the time, a repeat spot comes down to one or more of these:

  • A hormone-sensitive zone. Some areas, especially the chin and jawline, often react more to monthly hormone shifts.
  • A pore that never fully cleared. The visible spot heals, but the blockage underneath may not, so it fills up again.
  • Something that keeps touching that patch. Hands, phones, masks, helmet straps and hair products all tend to hit the same places every day.
  • Picking. Squeezing a spot can push it deeper and make it last longer, which keeps the area irritated.

These often overlap. A chin you rest on your hand in the week before your period gets several at once.

Hormone-sensitive zones: why the lower face

Everyone has androgens such as testosterone, and your oil glands respond to them. Research suggests breakouts happen partly because oil glands are extra sensitive to normal hormone levels, not because levels are unusual [1]. Those glands then make more oil, which mixes with skin cells and can clog pores [1].

In adult women, breakouts commonly appear on the lower face and neck, and flares often line up with the menstrual cycle [1]. In one survey of adult women seeing a dermatologist, 65% said their breakouts got worse around their period. Of those, 56% said the week before their period was the worst [2].

So if your repeat spot shows up on your chin or jawline in the days before you bleed, that fits a common pattern. It may not be the exact same pore each time. It may be the same zone reacting to the same point in your cycle.

Some people notice spots in mirror-image places on both sides of the jaw. A pattern on both sides may point more toward a whole-zone influence, like hormones, than a single habit. A spot that only ever appears on the side you hold your phone points the other way.

A pore that never fully cleared

Every breakout starts as a microcomedone, a tiny plug of skin cells and oil inside a pore. It's too small to see. Over time it can grow into a whitehead, a blackhead or an inflamed red spot [1].

When researchers tracked spots on people's faces with photos taken every two weeks, they found that most inflamed spots grew out of an existing clogged pore. A sizeable share appeared on skin that had looked normal two weeks earlier [3]. In other words, a lot of what surfaces as a red spot was already brewing underneath.

That helps explain the repeat spot. The red, sore part may settle after a few days, but the blockage underneath doesn't always clear completely. When oil output rises again before your next period, that same partly blocked pore can fill up and flare. To you, it looks like the same pimple coming back.

Research hasn't measured how often this happens, so treat it as a likely explanation, not a proven rule.

A repeat spot is often a timing and location story: the same zone, the same point in your cycle, and sometimes the same pore that never fully cleared.

Habits and contact: what keeps touching that spot

Not every repeat spot is hormonal. Some are simply where your skin gets touched, pressed or rubbed most. Dermatologists note that touching your face throughout the day can make breakouts flare [4]. A few common culprits:

  • Hands and resting your chin. Leaning your chin or cheek on your hand while you work or scroll puts pressure and friction on the same small area, day after day.
  • Your phone. If you hold your phone against the same cheek or jaw for calls, that patch gets warmth and pressure in the same place.
  • Hair products along the hairline. Oils in shampoos, conditioners and styling products can find their way onto your skin and clog pores. That often shows up as small bumps along the hairline, upper forehead or back of the neck [6]. Oily hair can also lead to forehead spots [4].
  • Masks, helmets and straps. Anything that covers the skin and traps heat, sweat and oil, or rubs as you move, can irritate pores. Dermatologists have seen this with face masks, helmets and chin straps [7].

The clue here is precision. Hormone-timed spots tend to show up across a zone. Contact spots tend to show up exactly where the contact is, such as the side of your chin you lean on or the line where a strap sits. They may also show up whenever the habit does, not only before your period.

Why picking keeps it going

It's very tempting to deal with a repeat spot yourself, especially if you know it's coming. But squeezing or popping can push what's inside, such as oil, dead skin cells and bacteria, deeper into the skin. That increases inflammation and can make the spot more noticeable, more painful and more likely to scar [5].

Picking also tends to make spots take longer to clear and raises the risk of dark marks [4]. Scrubbing and picking may even encourage new spots in the same area [1]. So a spot you squeezed last month may be part of why that patch keeps flaring.

The gentler approach is to leave the spot alone, wash with a mild cleanser and your fingertips rather than a scrub, and skip harsh, drying products, which can irritate skin and make things worse [5].

If you find it hard to stop picking, you're not alone, and it's worth mentioning to a doctor or dermatologist.

What face maps get wrong

Search for "why do I get pimples in the same spot" and you'll soon find face maps. They split the face into zones and link each one to an organ: forehead to the liver or digestion, nose to the heart, cheeks to the stomach, and so on [8].

These organ links aren't supported by science [8]. A forehead spot isn't a message from your liver. The one part that does line up with research is the lower face: chin and jawline breakouts are commonly linked to hormones, though hormones can affect other areas too [8].

The honest version: location alone tells you a little. Location plus timing tells you much more. A chin spot that only appears in the week before your period suggests a cycle pattern. One that appears after a week of leaning on your hand suggests a habit.

How to track a recurring spot

The best way to work out what's behind your repeat spot is to watch it for two or three cycles. One cycle is rarely enough, because sleep, stress and products can overlap with your cycle. Keep it simple:

  1. A daily photo at the same time, in the same light, with a bare face. Include the angle where the spot appears.
  2. Your cycle day, with day 1 as the first day of full bleeding. When your period arrives, count back to see where the spot fell.
  3. Location by zone: forehead, hairline, cheeks, chin, jawline (left or right), around the mouth.
  4. What kind of spot: small bump, red spot, or deeper and sore.
  5. Contact and habits: calls, chin-resting, mask or helmet time, new hair products.
  6. Whether you picked it, and how long it took to settle.

After a few cycles, look for two things. Does the spot appear at roughly the same cycle day? And does it appear in exactly the same place, or across a wider zone? Same day and same zone points toward your cycle. Same place but any day points toward contact or a pore that keeps refilling.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

An occasional spot that returns to the same zone around your period is common and usually nothing to worry about. But some recurring bumps are worth getting checked. See a doctor or dermatologist if the bump in the same spot:

  • is deep or painful [5]
  • never comes to a head
  • keeps returning for months, even when you leave it alone
  • is leaving scars or dark marks
  • looks or feels different from your usual spots, such as a firm lump under the skin, or keeps growing

It's also worth booking an appointment if your breakouts are getting worse over time, your cycles are very irregular, or your skin is affecting how you feel day to day. A few months of photos with cycle days marked can make that appointment more useful.

Questions

Why do I get a pimple in the same spot every period?

Usually a mix of things. The lower face often reacts more to monthly hormone shifts, so the same zone tends to flare before your period. A clogged pore that never fully cleared can fill up again, and habits like resting your chin on your hand keep hitting the same patch. Tracking spot location and cycle day for two or three cycles shows which fits you.

Why do I get pimples in the same spot over and over?

Most inflamed spots grow out of a clogged pore, so a pore that stays partly blocked can keep producing new spots in one place. Repeated touching, friction from a phone, mask or strap, hair products near the hairline and picking can all keep a single area irritated. If a spot keeps returning for months, see a doctor or dermatologist.

Is getting a pimple on my chin every month hormonal?

It may be. Breakouts in adult women commonly appear on the lower face and often follow the menstrual cycle, and many people notice them in the week before their period. But chin spots can also come from resting your chin on your hand or from a mask or strap. Logging the spot and your cycle day for a few cycles helps you tell the difference.

Does the location of a pimple tell you which organ is affected?

No. Face maps that link spots on the forehead, nose or cheeks to the liver, heart or stomach aren't supported by science. Location can still be a useful clue, though. Lower-face spots often follow hormone timing, and spots in one small patch often point to something that touches that patch.

When should I see a doctor about a pimple that keeps coming back in the same place?

See a doctor or dermatologist if the bump is deep or painful, never comes to a head, keeps returning for months, leaves scars or dark marks, or looks or feels different from your usual spots, such as a firm lump under the skin. It's also worth booking an appointment if breakouts are affecting how you feel.
