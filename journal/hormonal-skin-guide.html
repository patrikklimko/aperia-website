Your skin during your menstrual cycle: the complete guide to hormonal skin

If your skin seems to run on its own schedule, with the same good week, the same flare week and the same spots month after month, this guide is for you. Here's how skin often changes during the menstrual cycle, phase by phase, why it happens, and how to read your own rhythm instead of guessing.

What "hormonal skin" actually means

"Hormonal skin" simply means skin that visibly responds to the natural rise and fall of your hormones. Everyone's skin does this to some degree. For some people it's dramatic, for others subtle. It isn't a flaw or a failure of your routine. It's your skin reacting to a rhythm that repeats every month.

That rhythm is your menstrual cycle. It starts on the first day of your period and ends the day before the next one. The average cycle is about 28 days, but anywhere from 21 to 35 days can be normal [1]. Hormones change across that time, and your oil glands and skin barrier change with them [2][5][6].

Period (days 1–5)

When your period starts, estrogen and progesterone are both low [1]. In a small study that measured women's skin every week, skin was driest between days 1 and 6 [2]. At the same time, many people notice last week's spots settling. That's why some people say their skin looks better on their period, even if it feels a little tight. More in what happens to your skin during your period.

Follicular (days 6–12)

Estrogen climbs through this phase [1]. Estrogen supports skin thickness, moisture and elasticity, and skin tends to get thinner and drier when estrogen is low over the long term [7]. For many people this is the calmest, most even stretch of the month. Breakouts that started forming before your period can still pop up early on, though.

Ovulation (days 13–15)

Around day 14 of a 28-day cycle, a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) releases an egg [1]. Estrogen is at its peak, and testosterone also tends to be highest around mid-cycle [4]. Skin often looks its best here, but oil may start to rise.

Luteal (days 16–21)

After ovulation, progesterone rises [1], and testosterone stays a little higher than early in the cycle [4]. In the weekly skin study, surface oil appeared highest on days 16 to 20 [2]. More oil plus built-up skin cells is the classic setup for clogged pores [6]. If you notice shine, see oily skin before your period.

Watch week (days 22–28)

Late in the cycle, estrogen and progesterone drop and your next period begins [1]. The same weekly study found the skin barrier weakest between days 22 and 26 [2]. This is the best-known flare window. In a survey of adult women seeing a dermatologist, 65% said their breakouts got worse around their period, and 56% of those said the week before was the worst [3].

Your best skin week and your flare week aren't luck. They're two points on the same hormonal wave.

The hormones behind the changes

Three hormones do most of the work.

  • Estrogen rises in the first half of your cycle and peaks just before ovulation [1]. It helps keep skin thicker, more elastic and better hydrated [7].
  • Progesterone takes over after ovulation and is highest in the luteal phase [1]. Skin flares have been noted to line up with peak progesterone levels, possibly because the skin barrier and immune defences shift as hormones cycle [5].
  • Testosterone and other androgens are present in everyone. Your oil glands respond to them by growing and making more oil [6]. Testosterone tends to peak around mid-cycle and stay a little higher into the luteal phase [4].

Together they create a monthly wave. We go deeper in estrogen, progesterone and your skin and why your breakouts follow your cycle.

Research on skin across the cycle is still limited. Many studies are small, and they don't always measure the same things. So the phase map above is a useful starting point, and your own skin is the final word.

One note: if you use hormonal birth control, your hormone pattern is different, so the phases may not apply in the same way.

Why skin changes lag behind your hormones

Every breakout starts as a tiny plug of oil and skin cells deep in a pore, called a microcomedone. It's far too small to see [6]. Over time it can grow into a blackhead, whitehead or red spot [6].

That takes days, sometimes longer. So the spot you see on day 25 may have started forming around ovulation or in the luteal phase. It's one reason "acne during ovulation and luteal phase" and "breakouts after my period" are such common searches. Your skin is often showing you last week's hormones, not today's.

Where cycle breakouts tend to show up

Many people notice cycle-linked breakouts along the chin and jawline, sometimes the lower cheeks. If your spots keep landing in that band, at the same point in your cycle, that's a useful clue. See why you break out on your chin and jawline.

The other inputs: sleep, stress and food

Your cycle is the biggest rhythm, but three everyday inputs stack on top of it: sleep, stress and food. Breakouts have been found to get worse during stressful periods such as exams [8]. A high-stress, low-sleep week landing on your watch week is often when skin looks its worst. Understanding all four together is what turns "why is this happening?" into "of course".

Is your skin actually hormonal? How to tell

Look for three things together:

  1. Timing that follows your cycle. The same phase, month after month.
  2. A familiar location, often the chin and jawline.
  3. A pattern that repeats over at least two or three cycles.

You can't tell from a single week. Products can cause breakouts at any point in your cycle too, so if a flare follows something new, read is my skincare breaking me out? For the full checklist, see how to tell if your breakouts are hormonal.

What tends to help

You can't change your cycle, and that's fine. The biggest wins usually aren't a new product every week. They're:

  • Consistency. A steady, gentle routine kept up all month.
  • Anticipation. Knowing when your watch week lands, so a spot on day 24 isn't a surprise.
  • Small, phase-aware tweaks. A little more moisture around your period, lighter textures when oil rises. See a skincare rhythm for every phase of your cycle.
  • Patience. Change one thing at a time, and give it a couple of cycles before you judge it. See how long it takes to see skin improvement.

How to read your own skin

All of this becomes real when you can see it. A simple log over two or three cycles is enough:

  1. A daily photo at the same time, in the same light, with a bare face.
  2. Your cycle day, with day 1 as the first day of full bleeding.
  3. New spots: how many, where and what kind.
  4. Oiliness and dryness on a simple 1–5 scale.
  5. Sleep, stress, food and products, in a word or two each.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

A monthly rhythm of better and worse skin days is common and often nothing to worry about. It's worth talking to a doctor or dermatologist if:

  • your breakouts are deep, painful or leaving scars or dark marks
  • your skin keeps getting worse over several months
  • your cycles are very irregular, or you often go months without a period
  • skin changes come with other changes, like new hair growth
  • your skin is affecting how you feel day to day

A few months of photos and cycle notes can make that appointment more useful.

Questions

How does skin change during the menstrual cycle?

For many people, skin feels drier around their period, calmer and more even as estrogen rises in the follicular phase, and at its best around ovulation. After ovulation, oil often picks up, and the week before a period is when breakouts most often appear. Everyone's timing is a little different.

What are the menstrual cycle phases for skin?

On a typical 28-day cycle, Aperia uses five skin phases: period (days 1 to 5), follicular (days 6 to 12), ovulation (days 13 to 15), early luteal (days 16 to 21) and the watch week before your period (days 22 to 28). Cycles vary, so these are estimates, not fixed dates.

What is hormonal skin?

Hormonal skin is skin that visibly responds to the natural rise and fall of your hormones across your cycle, often getting oilier and more breakout-prone in the second half of the month. It's a normal pattern, not a flaw.

How do I know if my breakouts are hormonal?

Look for timing that follows your cycle (often the week before your period), spots that cluster around the chin and jawline, and a pattern that repeats month after month. Tracking your skin alongside your cycle for two or three months is the clearest way to see it.

Why is my skin worse at certain times of the month?

Your hormones change across the cycle, and your oil glands and skin barrier respond to them. Oil tends to rise after ovulation, and the skin barrier may be weaker in the days before your period. Sleep, stress, food and products can stack on top of that, which is why some months feel worse than others.

Does hormonal skin affect men?

Yes. Hormones influence oil and breakouts in everyone. Men don't have a monthly cycle, but hormonal shifts still affect their skin.
