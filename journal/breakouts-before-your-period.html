Why do I break out before my period? Your skin before your period, explained

If your skin flares in the days before your period, with a little more oil and a few spots in the same familiar places, you're noticing one of the most common patterns in skin. Here's what's going on, how many days before your period it usually starts, how long it lasts, and why it tends to land in the same spots.

What happens to your skin before your period

If you break out in the days before your period, it isn't a coincidence, and it isn't something you did wrong that week. It's one of the most predictable things skin does. In a survey of 400 women, 44% said their breakouts flared before their period [2]. In a later survey of adult women at a dermatology clinic, 65% said their breakouts were linked to their cycle, and more than half of them said the week before their period was the worst [1].

Hormones shift in the second half of your cycle

Around and after ovulation, estrogen and progesterone rise. Once your period begins, they drop for a few days before starting to climb again [5]. Androgens such as testosterone are present in everyone, and they stimulate receptors in your oil glands and hair follicles. That can lead to a build-up of oil and skin cells inside the pore, the starting point of a breakout [1].

Oil picks up, and the barrier may dip

A small study measured women's facial skin every week for two to three months. Oil on the skin's surface appeared highest around days 16 to 20 of the cycle, and the skin barrier was at its weakest between days 22 and 26 [4]. Together, that may leave skin shinier, more reactive and quicker to clog before a period.

The pore lining may swell

The NCBI's StatPearls review of breakouts notes that the pre-period flare seems to follow swelling in the lining of the pore, and that it's reported in about 70% of female patients [3].

The pre-period breakout isn't random. For many people, it's the most scheduled thing their skin does.

What "PMS skin" looks like

"PMS skin" and "PMS face" aren't medical terms, but they describe something real. Cleveland Clinic lists breakout flare-ups among the most common physical signs of PMS, alongside bloating, fatigue and headaches. Its symptoms tend to start one to two weeks before a period and usually ease once bleeding begins [5].

On your face, it often shows up as some mix of:

  • More shine, especially through the middle of the face, from the oil increase in the second half of the cycle [4]. Our guide to oily skin before your period goes deeper on this.
  • New spots, often deeper, tender ones along the lower face and jawline [1].
  • Skin that feels more reactive, stinging more easily or looking a little red, which may tie in with the barrier dip [4].
  • A puffy or tired look. Bloating, trouble sleeping and food cravings are all common PMS symptoms [5], and some people notice them in their face too.

Stress makes your body produce more androgens, which stimulate oil glands [7], and Cleveland Clinic names stress and lack of sleep as things that can make hormone-linked breakouts worse [6]. More in luteal phase skin.

Timing: when pre-period breakouts start and stop

How many days before your period do breakouts start?

For most people, it's within the last week. In the dermatology-clinic survey, 91% of women with cycle-linked breakouts said their spots began within the seven days before their period started [1]. Some people notice changes earlier, closer to the one-to-two-week window Cleveland Clinic gives for PMS symptoms in general [5].

There's a catch, though. A spot you see on day 25 didn't start on day 25. Most red spots begin as a clogged pore, often too small to notice. In one study that tracked 25 people's faces with photos every two weeks, 54% of red spots had started as a visible whitehead or blackhead, and 28% appeared from skin that had looked normal in the previous photo [8].

How long does period acne last?

A single spot can last anywhere from a few days to a few weeks [6]. The flare as a whole often peaks around the day your period starts, then tails off. Of the women in the clinic survey who had pre-period flares, 17% said their skin was actually worst during their period [1], usually because spots that formed the week before were still at their most visible.

When does period acne go away?

For most people, by the week after their period. In the same survey, 77% said their breakouts were gone within one week of their period ending [1]. If yours linger, see our guide to breakouts after your period.

Here's how that often looks across a typical 28-day cycle. Yours may run earlier or later.

  • Days 15–21: oil starts climbing after ovulation. Clogged pores may be forming, but you can't see much yet.
  • Days 21–28: the classic flare window. New spots surface, often on the lower face.
  • Days 1–3: spots peak or start to turn. Skin may feel drier as hormones drop [4].
  • Days 4–12: most pre-period spots fade, and many people find their skin settles.

Location: chin, jaw, cheeks, forehead, around the mouth

Pre-period breakouts often cluster in the same zones each month. Once you know your own map, a spot there in that week stops being a mystery. Face-mapping charts that link each zone to an organ have little research behind them, though.

Chin and jawline

This is the area most people associate with cycle breakouts. Adult breakouts in women are often described as deeper, tender spots on the lower third of the face, the jawline and the neck [1]. Read more in chin and jawline breakouts.

Cheeks

Cheeks are common too. Cleveland Clinic says hormone-linked breakouts are most likely to appear on the cheeks [6]. Phones, pillowcases and hands touch them a lot too.

Forehead

In the clinic survey, the forehead and cheeks were equally common spots (42% each) [1]. Forehead spots that sit along the hairline are worth checking against your hair products. The American Academy of Dermatology suggests making sure hair and skin products are labeled non-comedogenic, oil-free or "won't clog pores" [7].

Around the mouth

Spots around the mouth showed up in 31% of the survey group [1]. If the bumps around your mouth are tiny, clustered, flaky or stinging, and don't follow your cycle, show them to a dermatologist. Not every bump is a breakout.

The key finding from that survey is easy to miss: there was no meaningful difference in where spots appeared for women with cycle-linked breakouts compared with everyone else [1]. Location can hint at a pattern, but timing tells you much more.

Before your period, or another part of your cycle?

Not everyone's cycle breakouts land in the final week. In the clinic survey, 56% of women with cycle-linked breakouts said the week before their period was worst, 17% said during it, 3% said after it, and 24% said their skin was worse throughout the cycle [1].

  • Spots around mid-cycle, about two weeks before your period, may line up with ovulation. See breakouts during ovulation.
  • Spots in the last week, fading once bleeding starts or soon after, fit the classic pre-period pattern.
  • Spots that keep coming after your period may be a lag, or something else. See breakouts after your period.

If you use hormonal birth control, your natural hormone swings are usually flattened, so your pattern may look different or barely show up. And a common question: skin changes can't tell you whether you're pregnant; a test can.

It isn't always your cycle

Before you blame hormones, look at what else changed in the days before a flare:

  • Stress from work, exams or a tough week [7].
  • Sleep: a run of short or broken nights [6].
  • Products: a new moisturizer, sunscreen, makeup or hair product [7].
  • Food and drink: anything unusual for you.
  • Picking: squeezing or picking at spots can make hormone-linked breakouts worse [6].

These overlap with your cycle, so one month rarely tells the full story.

How to read your own rhythm

The most useful thing isn't a new product bought in a panic the day a spot appears. It's seeing the timing. Once you know a flare tends to arrive in a specific week, you can stop blaming yourself and keep a steady routine instead of overhauling it every time a spot appears.

To find your pattern, track two or three cycles:

  1. A daily photo at the same time, in the same light, with a bare face.
  2. Your cycle day, with day 1 as the first day of full bleeding. When your period arrives, count back to see how many days before it your spots began.
  3. New spots: how many, where (chin, jaw, cheeks, forehead, around the mouth) and what kind.
  4. Oiliness on a simple 1–5 scale.
  5. The extras: sleep, stress, new products and anything unusual you ate.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

A monthly rhythm of better and worse skin is common and often nothing to worry about. It's worth talking to a doctor or dermatologist if:

  • your breakouts are deep, painful or leaving scars or dark marks
  • your skin keeps getting worse over several months, or nothing you try seems to help
  • your cycles are very irregular, or breakouts come with other changes like new hair growth
  • you think a medicine might be playing a part (keep taking it, and ask the prescriber) [7]

The AAD notes that breakouts can sometimes be a sign of an underlying medical condition [7]. A few months of photos and cycle notes can make that appointment much more useful.

Key takeaways

  • Breaking out before your period is one of the most common skin patterns. In surveys, between about 44% and 65% of women report cycle-linked breakouts.
  • Most pre-period spots appear within the last seven days before bleeding, but they often start forming earlier as invisible clogged pores.
  • Many people find the flare peaks around day 1 and fades within a week of their period ending.
  • Chin, jaw, cheeks, forehead and around the mouth are all common. Location hints at a pattern, but timing tells you more.
  • Stress, sleep and products can stack on top of the pre-period week, so log them too.
  • See a doctor or dermatologist for breakouts that are painful, scarring or getting worse.
