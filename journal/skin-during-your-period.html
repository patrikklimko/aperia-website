Period skin: what happens to your skin during your period

Your skin runs a fairly predictable arc across the month, and your period is its own chapter. Here's what period skin often looks like day by day, why it can feel dry, itchy or dull, why spots from last week linger, and when things usually settle.

Quick answer

Period skin varies, but a common pattern is drier, more sensitive skin in the first days of bleeding, leftover spots from the week before, then calmer skin as your period ends. What happens to your skin during your period

Your period is the start of a new cycle. Day 1 is the first day of bleeding, and most people bleed for three to seven days [1]. If you didn't get pregnant, estrogen and progesterone drop just before your period, and the lining of the uterus sheds [1]. Estrogen stays low during your period [2], then starts climbing again through the follicular phase, which begins on the same day your period does [2].

That matters for your face because estrogen plays a part in collagen production and moisture in your skin [2]. When it's low, many people notice their skin feels less plump, drier or a bit more reactive. At the same time, the breakouts that formed in the week before your period are often still visible. So period skin is usually a mix of two things: the tail end of the pre-period flare, and a short low-hormone stretch.

Not everyone follows the textbook. Some people find their skin settles the moment bleeding starts. Others feel it's at its worst. Both are common, and which one is yours is worth knowing.

Skin before your period and skin during it are two different states. Most people only remember the bad week.

Period skin, day by day

Here's how the period days often unfold. Exact timing varies from person to person and month to month, so think of it as a rough map.

Days 1–2: leftover spots, drier skin

Spots that surfaced in the last week of your cycle may still be at their most visible. In a survey of adult women with cycle-linked breakouts, 17% said their skin was actually worst during their period [5]. A small study that measured facial skin every week found it was driest between days 1 and 6 of the cycle [3]. And skin may be more reactive now: one study found skin reacted more strongly to an irritant on day 1 than on days 9 to 11 [4].

Days 3–5: the turn

New breakouts usually slow down. Older spots start to fade, though some may leave red or dark marks for a while. Estrogen begins to rise as the follicular phase gets going [2], and some people start to notice their skin looking calmer.

The last days and just after

This is when many people's skin settles. In the same survey, 77% of women with pre-period flares said their breakouts were gone within a week of their period ending [5]. If spots keep coming well after that, our guide to breakouts after your period can help you work out why.

Dry, tight or flaky skin during your period

Dry skin during your period is one of the most common things people notice, and it lines up with the research. In the weekly-measurement study, skin was driest from about day 1 to day 6 [3]. With estrogen low [2], your skin may hold on to water a little less well.

Your routine can add to it. If you reached for drying spot products during a pre-period flare, your skin may feel tight or start flaking a few days later. That "skin shedding" feeling around your period is often just dryness and irritation catching up.

The American Academy of Dermatology's general tips for dry skin can help through this stretch [6]:

  • keep showers short and use warm, not hot, water
  • wash with a gentle cleanser, and don't overdo the lather
  • apply a fragrance-free moisturizer while skin is still slightly damp
  • if skin feels very dry, a cream may hold moisture better than a lotion

Itchy or sensitive skin during your period

Some people find their skin stings, itches or flushes more easily during their period. There's some research behind that. In a study of 29 women, skin reacted more strongly to a standard irritant on the first day of the cycle than on days 9 to 11, with more redness, swelling and water loss [4]. It's a small study, but it fits what many people describe.

Dryness plays a part too. The AAD notes that very dry skin can itch, and suggests asking a dermatologist before using anti-itch creams, because some contain ingredients that can make dryness and itch worse [6].

In practice, the first days of your period may not be the best time to try a new active ingredient or a strong exfoliant. If you want to test a new product, the week after your period may give you a fairer read. If you get itching that's intense, spreads beyond your face, comes with a rash or doesn't follow your cycle, show it to a doctor or dermatologist.

Why does my skin look bad on my period?

If your skin looks dull, flat or just "off" during your period, you're not imagining it. A few things often stack up:

  • Low-hormone dryness. Skin is often driest in the first days of the cycle [3], and dry skin tends to look less bright.
  • Leftover breakouts. Spots from the week before may still be red, healing or leaving marks [5].
  • Poor sleep. Trouble sleeping is a common symptom around your period [1], and it can show on your face.
  • Stress. Cramps and a heavy week can add up. Stress makes the body produce more androgens, which stimulate oil glands [7].
  • Comfort food and less movement, which are normal during a period and can make you feel puffier or more tired.

Most of this is temporary. For many people, the dullness lifts as estrogen rises later in the week [2].

Why some people's skin looks better on their period

The opposite is common too. Plenty of people notice a glow in the last days of bleeding or just after. This page covers what happens across all the period days. If you want to know why skin can look better on your period, and when that glow really peaks, read our guide: why your skin looks better on your period.

The short version: by the end of your period, the pre-period flare has often faded and estrogen is climbing again. That combination can leave skin looking calmer and brighter, even if day 1 felt rough.

Gentle care for period week

You don't need a separate routine for your period, but a few small shifts may help skin feel more comfortable:

  • Keep it simple. A gentle cleanser, a moisturizer and sunscreen in the morning.
  • Go easy on spot products. Use them on the spot, not all over, so you don't add to the dryness.
  • Hold off on new things. Try new products after your period, when skin may be less reactive [4].
  • Leave spots alone as much as you can, so already-reactive skin isn't irritated further.
  • Sleep where you can. It's one of the few inputs you can control in a heavy week.

See your own arc

Because everyone's timing is different, the most useful thing you can do is watch your own skin for two or three cycles:

  1. A daily photo at the same time, in the same light, with a bare face.
  2. Your cycle day, with day 1 as the first day of full bleeding.
  3. Quick ratings for dryness, oiliness, itch or sensitivity, and new spots.
  4. The extras: sleep, stress, new products and anything unusual you ate.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

Some skin changes around your period are common and usually nothing to worry about. It's worth seeing a doctor or dermatologist if:

  • your breakouts are deep, painful or leaving scars or dark marks
  • dryness or itching doesn't ease after your period, or keeps getting worse
  • you get a rash, hives or itching that spreads beyond your face
  • your periods are very irregular, very heavy or very painful [1]
  • your skin is affecting how you feel day to day

Key takeaways

  • Period skin is often a mix of leftover pre-period spots and a short low-hormone stretch.
  • Estrogen and progesterone are low at the start of a period. One small study found skin driest around days 1 to 6.
  • Skin may be more reactive on day 1, so it can be a good time to keep your routine simple.
  • Dull or "bad" period skin often comes from dryness, healing spots, poor sleep and stress together.
  • Many people find their skin settles in the last days of their period or the week after.
  • See a doctor or dermatologist for breakouts that are painful or scarring, or for itching or dryness that doesn't ease.

Questions

Why do I break out during my period?

Most period breakouts actually start in the days before your period, when oil picks up and pores clog more easily. Spots that formed then are often most visible around day 1 or 2. In one survey, most women with cycle-linked breakouts said their spots were gone within a week of their period ending.

Does skin get better during your period?

For some people, yes. Skin often starts to calm once the pre-period flare passes, especially in the last days of bleeding. Others find it gets drier or more sensitive in the first days, because estrogen and progesterone are low. Both are common, and tracking a few cycles shows which pattern is yours.

Why is my skin dry during my period?

Estrogen and progesterone are both low at the start of a period, and one small study found facial skin was driest around days 1 to 6 of the cycle. Drying spot products used during a pre-period flare can add to it. A gentle cleanser and a fragrance-free moisturizer may help skin feel more comfortable.

Why is my skin itchy or sensitive during my period?

One small study found skin reacted more strongly to an irritant on the first day of the cycle than on days 9 to 11. Dryness can also make skin itch. Keeping your routine simple during your period may help. If itching is intense, spreads beyond your face or comes with a rash, see a doctor or dermatologist.

Why does my skin look bad or dull on my period?

Several things often stack up: low-hormone dryness, spots from the week before that are still healing, poor sleep and the stress of a heavy week. Dry skin tends to look less bright. For many people, dullness lifts as estrogen rises again toward the end of their period.

When is skin clearest in the cycle?

Many people find their skin calmest in the follicular phase, the week or so after their period, when estrogen is rising and pre-period spots have faded. Timing varies from person to person, so a few cycles of daily photos and notes are the best way to find your own best-skin window.
