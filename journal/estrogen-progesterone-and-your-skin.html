Why does progesterone cause acne? Estrogen, progesterone and your skin, explained

If your skin has a monthly rhythm, two hormones get most of the credit and most of the blame: estrogen and progesterone. Here's what each one does, what the research really says about progesterone and breakouts, and how to see your own pattern.

Two hormones, one monthly wave

You don't need a biology degree to understand your skin's monthly rhythm. You just need a rough feel for what estrogen and progesterone do, and when each one takes the lead.

Estrogen climbs through the first half of your cycle, after your period, and peaks around ovulation. After ovulation, the empty follicle turns into a temporary gland called the corpus luteum, which starts making progesterone [4]. Progesterone stays high through the second half of the cycle, the luteal phase. If you don't get pregnant, the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone drops, and your period starts [4].

A third player sits in the background: androgens such as testosterone. Everyone has them. Your oil glands carry androgen receptors, and androgens stimulate them to make more oil (sebum) [5]. In one study that measured hormones across the cycle, testosterone peaked at mid-cycle and stayed higher into the middle of the luteal phase [6].

Your skin responds to the balance of all three, not to one hormone in isolation. That's the key to the progesterone question.

Estrogen skin changes: the first half

Estrogen is often called skin's good-weather hormone, and the research mostly backs that up. It's linked to thicker skin, more collagen, better hydration and a stronger skin barrier [7]. Researchers also generally agree that estrogen holds oil production back [1].

That's why many people notice their skin looks calmer and more even in the week or two after their period, as estrogen climbs. If you've ever wondered why your best skin days seem to land mid-month, this is a big part of it. Our guide on why skin can look better on your period covers the early part of that window.

Estrogen changes don't only happen across a cycle. Levels also shift over a lifetime, which is one reason skin texture and dryness can change with age [7]. This article sticks to the monthly rhythm.

Why does progesterone cause breakouts?

Progesterone may not be the direct cause. After ovulation, progesterone rises while estrogen, which tends to calm oil glands, is past its peak. Oil output often climbs in the luteal phase, and androgens such as testosterone stimulate oil glands. Research on progesterone's own effect on human oil glands is mixed, so the shifting balance is the likelier explanation.

Here's what the evidence actually looks like, because this is where a lot of online advice gets ahead of the science.

The timing fits

In a small study that measured oil output across the cycle, oil fell through the first half, reached its lowest point a few days after ovulation, rose again during the luteal phase, then dipped just before the period [1]. A separate study that measured women's skin weekly found oil output highest around days 16 to 20 [8]. Both line up with the stretch when progesterone is high. Skin flares have also been noted to line up with peak progesterone [3].

But timing isn't proof

The same researchers who tracked that luteal rise in oil wrote plainly that progesterone's effect on the oil gland is controversial [1]. Some older studies found progesterone increased oil. Others found that progesterone at normal body levels had no effect on oil production in humans [1]. A dermatology survey on pre-period breakouts reached a similar place: the link with the cycle is clear, but the exact mechanism is still unknown [2].

The commonly cited explanation

The explanation you'll see most often is about balance rather than a single hormone. In the second half of the cycle, estrogen's oil-calming effect is weaker than at its mid-cycle peak, while androgens keep stimulating the oil glands [1][5][6]. Progesterone rises alongside those changes, so it gets the blame. It may play a part, but on current evidence it's more accurate to call it one piece of a shifting mix.

Progesterone arrives at the scene of most second-half breakouts. That doesn't mean it's the only one responsible.

What the second half of your cycle looks like

The luteal phase is roughly the two weeks between ovulation and your next period. For many people, skin changes in waves across it:

  • Just after ovulation: oil may start to pick up. Some people notice their first spots of the month here. Our guide to breakouts during ovulation covers this mid-cycle wave.
  • Mid-luteal: progesterone is near its peak and oil output often stays higher [1][8].
  • The last week before your period: this is the classic flare window. In one survey of adult women, 65% said their breakouts were worse around their period, and most of them said the week before was the worst [2].

Why would breakouts flare just as oil dips before a period? One old idea is that fluid retention in the days before a period may press on the pore's opening, making it harder for oil to flow out [1]. That's a theory, not a settled fact.

There's also a lag to keep in mind. Every spot starts as a tiny clogged pore that's too small to see, and it takes time to become visible [5]. So a spot you notice two days before your period may have started forming a week or more earlier. Progesterone acne on the chin and jaw

"Progesterone acne chin" is a common search, and there's a reason. Breakouts in adult women often show up on the lower third of the face, along the jawline and on the neck, and they tend to be deeper, inflamed red spots [2]. Many people notice these before their period.

But location on its own isn't proof that a breakout is hormonal. In the same survey of adult women, spots appeared across the forehead, cheeks and around the mouth, and there was no significant difference in location between women who had pre-period flares and those who didn't [2]. Chin spots can also come from phones, hands, masks, helmet straps or a new product.

The more useful clue is timing. If chin breakouts keep appearing in the same week of your cycle, month after month, that's a stronger signal than location. Our article on chin and jawline breakouts goes deeper.

Estrogen and skin sensitivity

Some people notice their skin stings or reacts more easily in certain weeks. Estrogen is usually linked to a stronger skin barrier, not a weaker one [3][7]. When the barrier is weaker, skin can feel drier and more easily irritated.

In a small study that measured skin weekly, the barrier was slightly weaker in the late luteal phase, around days 22 to 26, when estrogen and progesterone both fall before a period [8]. The change was small, though, and not everyone will feel it. Reviews of the menstrual cycle and skin suggest that cyclical hormone shifts may lower barrier and immune defences around the period, which may help explain why some skin issues flare then [3].

If your skin often feels more reactive in one particular week, that's worth noting. It may be a week to skip a new active ingredient and keep your routine simple.

How to see your own rhythm

Everyone's hormone balance and skin sensitivity are a little different, so the most useful step is to watch your own pattern for two or three cycles. Keep it simple:

  1. A daily photo at the same time, in the same light, with a bare face.
  2. Your cycle day, with day 1 as the first day of full bleeding.
  3. Oiliness on a simple 1 to 5 scale.
  4. New spots: how many, where, and what kind.
  5. How your skin feels: tight, stingy, calm or reactive.
  6. The extras: sleep, stress, food and any new products, since these can look like hormone effects.

When your next period starts, look back at the two weeks before it. Did oil and spots rise in the luteal phase? Did they ease once your period began? That's your own estrogen-and-progesterone rhythm, with dates attached.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

A monthly rhythm of calmer and oilier weeks is common and often nothing to worry about. It's worth talking to a doctor or dermatologist if:

  • your breakouts are deep, painful or leaving scars or dark marks
  • your skin keeps getting worse over several months
  • your cycles are very irregular, or you often go months without a period
  • breakouts come with other changes, like new hair growth on the face or body
  • your skin is affecting how you feel day to day

Bring a few months of photos and cycle notes. They can make the conversation much more useful.

Key takeaways

  • Estrogen leads in the first half of your cycle and is linked to calmer, better-hydrated skin and less oil.
  • Progesterone rises after ovulation, the same stretch when oil output often climbs.
  • Progesterone's direct effect on human oil glands is debated. The shifting balance of estrogen, progesterone and androgens is the more common explanation.
  • Pre-period breakouts are common, but the exact mechanism is still unknown.
  • Chin and jaw spots are common in adult women, but timing across cycles says more than location.
  • Track photos, cycle day, oil and spots for two or three cycles to see your own rhythm.
